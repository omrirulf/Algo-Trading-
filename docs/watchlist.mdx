The watchlist lives in `config/instruments.py`, not in an environment variable,
so a change to what gets traded shows up in a diff — the same reasoning that
keeps the guardrail thresholds in code.

It is split into two sleeves that are **different bets and get different
position caps**.

```python
from config.instruments import SINGLE_NAMES, INDEX_SLEEVE, kind_for
from config.watchlist import DEFAULT_WATCHLIST
```

## Why both, instead of picking one

Diversification and edge pull in opposite directions here, and neither argument
settles it:

- **Breadth argues for indices.** One call on `IWM` prices two thousand small
  caps. Reaching that breadth through single names would cost a hundred times
  as much and drown in thin news coverage. Small caps, REITs, duration and
  actual commodities have no affordable single-name route at all.
- **Edge argues for single names.** Only a specific company has the analyst
  coverage and insider filings three of the five dimensions are built on. A
  sentiment read on a broad index is *macro timing* — the hardest call in
  markets, and the one where a language model reading a day of headlines has
  least obvious advantage.

<Note>
  So both ship, and `backtest/compare_sleeves.py` decides between them from
  real bars rather than from the paragraph above. Run it from the **compare
  sleeves** workflow in the Actions tab — it needs no credentials.
</Note>

### What replaced the old list

The previous 30 names were **37% technology**, and called their ADRs
geographic diversification. They were not: ASML, TSM, SAP and INFY are global
technology cyclicals that fall with US technology in a drawdown, whatever
exchange they list on. A mega-cap multinational's listing venue tells you
almost nothing about what it correlates with.

Technology is now 18% of the single-name sleeve, no sector exceeds 25%, and the
cap tiers and asset classes that were missing entirely — small caps, REITs,
duration, commodities themselves rather than the companies that dig them up —
arrive through the index sleeve.

## Two caps, and why the second one is not just bigger

| Instrument | Cap per position | Constant |
|---|---|---|
| Single name | 5% | `MAX_POSITION_PCT` |
| Index fund | 12% | `MAX_ETF_POSITION_PCT` |
| **Everything, combined** | **60%** | `MAX_GROSS_EXPOSURE_PCT` |

A broad fund is already hundreds of positions, so holding one at the
single-name cap buys 5% of market exposure and leaves the account in
cash — the diversification argument for indices and a 5% cap cancel each
other out.

But a larger cap is not automatically a safer one. **Risk per trade scales as
`cap / volatility`**, so a cap four times larger on an instrument only twice as
quiet carries roughly twice the planned risk. A first pass at 20% measured
~1.9× the per-trade risk of a single name — bigger, not safer. 12% keeps it
near parity while still buying enough exposure to matter.

<Warning>
  **`MAX_GROSS_EXPOSURE_PCT` is the one guardrail that got looser.** It used to
  be implicit at 50% (`MAX_OPEN_POSITIONS × MAX_POSITION_PCT`) and is now an
  explicit 60%, leaving a 40% cash floor. Without it the per-ticker caps
  multiply out to leverage: ten positions at the ETF cap would be twice the
  account.
</Warning>

## The instrument kind is a privilege boundary

The ETF cap is larger than the single-name cap, which makes the classification
something a model must never control. If a signal could assert *"this is a
fund"*, it could enlarge its own position by claiming one.

So it cannot:

- The kind is resolved from `config/instruments.py`, keyed on the ticker.
- `LLMSignal` has no field that could carry one, and `extra="forbid"` rejects
  an invented one as a validation error before any execution logic runs.
- An unrecognised ticker resolves to `EQUITY` — the **tighter** cap — so a
  misspelling can only ever size down.
- Two CI invariants enforce it: the schema stays closed to anything
  kind-shaped, and `MAX_ETF_POSITION_PCT` is readable only where it is defined
  and applied.

## What this cannot reach

Alpaca trades `us_equity`, `us_option` and `crypto` only — confirmed from the
SDK's own `AssetClass` enum. There is **no path** to the Frankfurt, Paris,
London or Tel Aviv order books, and none to futures. Everything above is
US-listed, which is why regional exposure comes through US-listed funds and
ADRs rather than local shares.

<Note>
  `EIS` (Israel) is the thinnest holding here by volume. Wide spreads are a
  real execution cost on a fund that size; `MIN_ATR_PCT_OF_PRICE` guards the
  degenerate case but not the ordinary one.
</Note>

## What the funds give up

Two of the five dimensions are **structurally absent** for a fund, not missing:
nobody publishes a price target on an index, and a fund has no insiders filing
Form 4. That distinction is carried through the whole pipeline rather than
papered over:

- `orchestrator/context.py` omits those sections entirely instead of rendering
  them as "unavailable this cycle" — a section that was never there is not a
  data gap, and should not invite the model to wonder what it missed.
- A separate `ETF_SYSTEM_PROMPT` asks a macro question instead of a company
  one, states that the two dimensions do not exist, and **sets a higher bar for
  a non-neutral call** because macro timing is harder than stock selection.
- The news query changes too: `"IWM ETF"` rather than `"IWM stock"`.

## Cost

| Watchlist size | Rough Claude spend / month |
|---|---|
| 10 | ~$69 |
| 20 | ~$138 |
| **32 (default)** | **~$221** |

<Warning>
  **These are estimates, and the assumed output length is the whole uncertainty
  in them.** Output dominates the bill, so guessing it wrong moves the total
  proportionally. Once you have run real cycles the journal carries measured
  `usage` blocks — use those instead, and see [Cost](/cost).
</Warning>

The index sleeve is the cost-efficient half: one call on `VWO` prices an entire
emerging-market basket, where the same breadth in single names would be dozens
of calls. Trimming the list is one lever, but **effort is cheaper to turn than
length** — see [Cost](/cost).

<Warning>
  **A longer list does not mean more exposure.** `MAX_OPEN_POSITIONS` caps
  holdings at 10, the per-ticker caps bound each one, and
  `MAX_GROSS_EXPOSURE_PCT` bounds all of them together. Breadth buys the model
  more to choose between, not a larger book — see [Guardrails](/guardrails).
</Warning>

## Overriding it

`WATCHLIST` still works and takes precedence, for a quick experiment:

```bash
WATCHLIST=IWM,GLD,MSFT python -m orchestrator.heartbeat --once
```

<Note>
  An override changes *what is evaluated*, never how it is sized. A ticker
  reached this way is still classified by `config/instruments.py`, so anything
  it does not recognise gets the tighter single-name cap.
</Note>

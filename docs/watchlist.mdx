What gets traded lives in `config/instruments.py`, version-controlled rather
than in an environment variable, so a change shows up in a diff — the same
reasoning that keeps the guardrail thresholds in code.

**Funds are the core holding and single names the satellite.** That ordering
is deliberate: a broad fund is diversified by construction, while a
stock-picking edge is unproven here, and the sleeve budgets decline to assume
one.

## Sleeve 1 — single names (16)

The only holdings where analyst coverage and insider filings exist, so the
only ones where all five prompt dimensions have content.

| Sector | Tickers |
|---|---|

| Communication | GOOGL |
| Financials | JPM, RY, HDB |
| Health care | LLY, NVO, TEVA |
| Industrials | CAT, ESLT |
| Consumer discretionary | TM, MELI |
| Consumer staples | PG |
| Energy | XOM |

<Note>
  No materials sector any more. NEM and BHP were standing in for gold and
  copper, and a miner is a company bet with a commodity attached — company
  risk, management risk, and a hedging book on top. The metals are now held
  directly.
</Note>

## Sleeve 2a — broad funds (14)

A fund belongs here only if its holdings span **sectors, countries or
issuers**. One that holds a hundred companies inside a single sector, or a
single country, is a focused fund below and gets a smaller cap for it.

| Role | Tickers |
|---|---|
| US broad equity | RSP, IWM |
| International equity | VGK, VWO |
| Duration | SHY, IEF, TLT, TIP (+ `LQD` for exposure, sized as credit) |
| Credit | HYG, EMB (+ `LQD`, grouped with Duration) |
| Broad commodities | DBC, DBA |
| Currency | UUP |

<Note>
  **Both are sized here; neither is grouped here.** "Broad commodities" is
  a sizing roster, not an exposure group — both members are broad funds,
  and measurement showed neither is a broad *bet*.

  `DBA` holds corn, wheat, soybeans and sugar, the same four funds in
  sleeve 2c, so it counts against the **Agriculture** ceiling. `DBC` is
  roughly 55–60% energy futures and measures 0.82 excess correlation
  against the energy complex over 2007–present, so it counts against
  **Energy**. Both keep the broad-fund cap: ten crops and a
  three-sector basket genuinely are more diversified than one thing, and
  sizing asks a different question from grouping.

  Found the hard way, and worth knowing. Grouped together the two diluted
  each other, and the pair showed up as the only one of 171 to breach
  |excess r| ≥ 0.6 — against Agriculture. Separating `DBA` fixed that and
  immediately revealed `DBC`'s energy character, which the averaging had
  hidden from the same [measurement](/guardrails) one run earlier.
</Note>

`RSP` rather than `SPY`: a cap-weighted US index is a concentrated technology
bet wearing a diversified label. Equal weight is the diversified version of
the same market.

**Rates are no longer one point on the curve.** `TLT` alone made every rate
story a bet on the long end. `SHY` is a cash proxy, `IEF` the belly, `TIP`
prices inflation rather than growth.

`LQD` sits in the **Duration** group with them, and `HYG` and `EMB` do not.
That split is by behaviour, not by label. Investment-grade credit is mostly a
rate instrument with a thin spread on top — it moves with the Treasury curve
— while high yield sells off with equities while government paper rallies.
Netting those two into one number would have hidden both. `LQD` is still
*sized* as credit; grouping and sizing ask different questions.

`UUP` is here because almost everything else on this list is priced in
dollars, so the dollar is a factor inside all of them and otherwise
unobservable.

## Sleeve 2b — focused funds (41)

One sector, or one country. **This is the sleeve that makes the watchlist
worth reading.** With only `RSP` and `IWM`, the strongest thing the model
could say was "US equities up" — macro timing, the hardest call on the list.
A rate cut, an oil shock, a drug approval or an election is a *sector* or a
*country* story, and until this sleeve existed there was nowhere for such a
story to land.

| Role | Tickers |
|---|---|
| Energy | XLE |
| Financials | XLF, KRE |
| Health care | XLV, XBI |
| Technology | XLK, XLC, SMH, IGV |
| Industrials | XLI, ITA, IYT |
| Consumer | XLY, XLP |
| Utilities | XLU |
| Materials | XLB |
| Real estate | VNQ, XHB |
| Miners | GDX |
| Developed countries | EWJ, EIS, EWU, EWG, EWL, EWN, EWI, EWP, EWD, EWC, EWA |
| Emerging countries | MCHI, INDA, EWY, EWT, EWZ, EWW, KSA, TUR, EZA, EPOL, ARGT |

<Warning>
  **A sector fund is diversified against one company failing and against
  nothing else.** `XLE` is twenty-three different ways of being long the oil
  price. A country fund is one currency, one central bank and one government.
  Sizing either like a fund that spans the world would repeat, one level up,
  the error the commodity cap exists to avoid — reading a cap off the word
  "fund" rather than off the risk.
</Warning>

Two holdings moved *down* a tier when this sleeve was created: `EWJ` and
`EIS` were broad funds at 12%, and `VNQ` with them. Japan is one country and
real estate is one sector; neither is a whole market.

<Note>
  **Overlap with the broad sleeve is deliberate and is not double counting.**
  `RSP` already contains every US sector, so a sector position is a *tilt* on
  top of the market. What stops the tilt and the single names beneath it
  adding up to one undiversified bet is the exposure group — see below, where
  `XLK`, `SMH`, `IGV`, `XLC`, `MSFT`, `NVDA`, `ASML` and `GOOGL` all share
  one 25% ceiling.
</Note>

## Sleeve 2c — the goods themselves (9)

| Role | Tickers |
|---|---|
| Precious metals | GLD, SLV |
| Industrial metals | CPER |
| Energy commodities | USO, UNG |
| Agriculture | CORN, WEAT, SOYB, CANE (+ `DBA` for exposure, sized above) |

Alpaca trades `us_equity`, `us_option` and `crypto` only, so futures are out
of reach and physical metal is not something a brokerage account can hold.
These US-listed funds are the only route to the commodities rather than the
companies that mine and drill them.

<Warning>
  **A single-commodity fund is not a diversified fund, and is capped below
  even a single stock.** Three risks pile on that no equity carries:

  1. **Roll decay.** A futures-backed fund rolls contracts monthly. In
     contango it sells cheap and buys dear every roll, so it can bleed value
     while spot is flat. `USO` is the notorious case. These are not
     buy-and-hold instruments.
  2. **Issuer credit risk.** `JO` is an exchange-traded **note** — unsecured
     debt of its issuer, not a fund holding anything. If the issuer fails the
     note can go to zero with coffee trading normally. ETNs can also be called
     by the issuer.
  3. **Thinness.** Several trade a small fraction of a broad fund's volume, so
     the spread is a real cost and a stop can fill badly.
</Warning>

### Rice and coffee have no single-commodity fund

**Rice**: no US-listed fund tracks it. Rough-rice futures exist; nothing wraps
them.

**Coffee**: `JO` was the route, and it stopped resolving. Both now reach the
portfolio only through `DBA`'s basket, if at all. These are limits of what
trades on a US exchange, not choices.

<Note>
  **Verified against live data on 2026-09-14** by the `market checks` workflow.
  35 of the 36 original tickers resolved. The one that did not was `JO`, the
  iPath coffee ETN — which is the ETN risk above arriving in practice, between
  the ticker being added and first being checked. It has been removed; coffee
  exposure now comes through `DBA`, whose basket includes it.
</Note>

## What each kind is asked about

Beyond the caps, the instrument kind now decides **what evidence the model is
shown**. A fund gets `FUND BASICS` in place of the company form, and the
commodities, bond funds and broad index funds carry a weekly `POSITIONING`
read from the CFTC — the nearest thing an index has to insider activity. See
[market context](/orchestrator/market-context#each-kind-of-ticker-is-asked-the-question-that-fits-it).

## Four kinds, four caps

| Kind | Cap | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Single name | 5% | One company, idiosyncratic: it can gap on a fraud or a failed trial. |
| Broad fund | 12% | Hundreds of positions across sectors, countries or issuers. Its tail is truncated in a way a company's is not. |
| **Focused fund** | **7%** | One sector or one country. Above a single name because a sector cannot go to zero on a fraud; below a broad fund because one sector is one bet. |
| Single-commodity fund | 4% | "Fund" does no diversification work — coffee is one thing — plus roll decay, issuer risk and thin volume. |

The ordering is the point: **the tightest cap belongs to the commodity funds,
not the stocks.** Sizing a coffee ETN like `RSP` because both are technically
ETFs would be picking a cap by label rather than by risk — and the focused
tier exists so that widening the list from ten funds to fifty-five did not
have to repeat that mistake one level up.

<Note>
  **The focused cap started at 8% and the measurement moved it down to 7%.**
  It shipped provisional, the `market checks` workflow measured it over two
  years of real bars, and 8% came back carrying *more* risk per trade than a
  single name at 5% — the exact thing the broad-fund cap had already been
  tuned down to avoid. Details below.
</Note>

### What the real data said about these caps

Risk per trade scales roughly as `cap / volatility`, so the caps are only
defensible against measured volatility. Three months of real bars, 2026-09-14:

| Kind | Median daily volatility | vs a single name |
|---|---|---|
| Single name | 2.25% | — |
| Broad fund | 0.47% | 0.21× |
| Focused fund | 1.16% | 0.52× |
| Single-commodity fund | 1.54% | 0.68× |

**The broad-fund cap checks out.** A single name is 4.8× as volatile as a
broad fund, so a broad-fund cap well above 12% would still carry no more risk
per trade than 5% on a stock. It started as a guess and is now checked twice.

### Volatility is the loose bound — the stop is what decides

`backtest/compare_sleeves.py` opens a mechanical trade every five bars across
each sleeve and runs it through the **real** ATR stop and sizing math. Two
years, 2026-09-15:

| Sleeve | Cap | Trades | Risk per trade | Worst trade |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Single name | 5% | 1,552 | **0.25%** | −0.97% |
| Broad fund | 12% | 1,263 | 0.21% | −0.85% |
| Focused fund | **8%** | 3,977 | **0.28%** | −1.09% |
| Single commodity | 4% | 873 | 0.18% | −0.82% |

**This is what moved the focused cap from 8% to 7%.** On median volatility
alone a focused fund is half as volatile as a single name, and 9.7% would have
been defensible. Through the actual stop it was not: at 8% a sector or country
position could lose 0.28% of the account, against 0.25% for a stock at 5%, and
its worst trade was worse. 7% is where that reaches parity — `8% × 0.25/0.28`.

The same disagreement, resolved the same way, is why single commodities sit at
4% when their volatility would allow ~7%. **Where two measurements disagree,
the one closer to what a position can actually lose wins.**

**The commodity cap is deliberately tighter than the arithmetic allows.**
Volatility alone would justify ~7%. It stays at 4% for three reasons the
median hides:

- **The spread inside the sleeve is wide.** `USO` measured 3.29% and `SLV`
  2.62% — both *above* the median single name. A cap set on the median would
  be far too loose at the volatile end.
- **Daily volatility cannot see roll decay.** A futures-backed fund can bleed
  value with spot flat, and no standard deviation captures that.
- **Nor issuer risk.** `JO` proved that one.

## The portfolio limits

Per-ticker caps bound one position. They say nothing about the shape of the
book, and that gap was real: nothing stopped the engine opening MSFT, NVDA,
TSM, ASML and GOOGL on one morning — five positions, one bet, every
per-ticker cap satisfied.

| Limit | Value | What it stops |
|---|---|---|
| Exposure group | 25% | A one-bet book. Spans every sleeve, so XOM + `XLE` + `USO` + `UNG` is one energy bet made four times. |
| Duration group | 30% | Its own ceiling, sized on the worst fill the per-position caps actually allow (three funds, not one): 6.8% of the account, calendar-2022 returns. |
| Net stock-market risk | 60% | The eleven equity groups each bound one sector or region; none bounds what they all share. Counted by beta, so a short offsets a long. |
| Single-name sleeve | 25% | Stock picking growing past its budget. |
| Fund sleeve | 70% | — the core allocation. |
| Gross exposure | 95% | Leverage, and nothing else. |

They all run **before any market-data fetch**, so a full book costs no quote,
and the tightest of them is passed into sizing as a ceiling — a partly-full
book sizes the next position *down* rather than placing it and rejecting it
after. A rejection names the limit that actually bound, so "no room under the
`'Energy'` group limit" is never reported as a gross-exposure problem.

The sleeve budgets sum to the gross cap, so gross binds only when a sleeve is
under-used rather than being a fourth independent limit.

### The shape this produces

A greedily-filled book, 100k account:

```
12 positions across 6 exposure groups
funds 74% of the book | names 26% | deployed 95% | cash 5%

Developed international  25%    Real estate  12%
US broad equity          24%    Duration      9%
Technology               20%    Financials    5%
```

No group exceeds its ceiling, and filling the book **requires** at least four
of them: being fully invested and being concentrated are now incompatible.
Eighteen exposure groups exist, so the four are easier to find — and the one
limit that grew, Duration's, grew because its members can lose less.

## The instrument kind is a privilege boundary

The caps differ by kind, which makes the classification something a model must
never control. If a signal could assert *"this is a broad fund"*, it could
enlarge its own position by claiming one.

- The kind resolves from `config/instruments.py`, keyed on the ticker.
- `LLMSignal` has no field that could carry one, and `extra="forbid"` turns an
  invented one into a validation error before any execution logic runs.
- An unrecognised ticker resolves to `EQUITY` — never the largest cap, and
  never the focused one either — so a misspelling can only size down. It also
  lands in its own exposure group, so it cannot consume another group's room.
- CI invariants enforce the schema stays closed, the fund caps are readable
  only where defined and applied, and every portfolio limit reaches the
  execution path.

## What the funds read instead

Nobody publishes a price target on an index or on copper, and neither has
insiders filing Form 4. For a while that meant a fund was scored on three
dimensions rather than five.

It no longer does. **A fund is scored on all five**, with the last two read
from what a fund actually has:

| Dimension | A company | A fund |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `analyst_score` | analyst ratings and price targets | **ANALYST VIEW OF THE HOLDINGS** — the ratings of the companies it holds, rolled up by weight, with a coverage figure saying what share of the fund that actually spans |
| `insider_score` | Form 4 filings | **POSITIONING** (what large speculators hold, from the CFTC's weekly report) or **FUND FLOWS** (shares created and destroyed, so money actually going in or out) |

Both substitutes are *behaviour that has already happened*, which is what the
insider slot was always for.

A score is left null only when the substitute is genuinely absent too — a bond
fund has no analyst roll-up, and a basket spanning a dozen contracts has no
single futures position. That is carried through rather than papered over:
`orchestrator/context.py` omits the section instead of rendering it
"unavailable this cycle", because a 0.00 on a missing section reads as
"neutral" when the truth is "there was nothing to read". A separate
`ETF_SYSTEM_PROMPT` asks a macro question and sets a higher bar for a
non-neutral call.

<Note>
  This page said the opposite until 16 Sep, and so did the signals artifact.
  Both were describing the version before the roll-up and the positioning
  block existed. The code had moved on; the explanations had not.
</Note>

<Warning>
  **That higher bar interacts with the fund-heavy budget, and the interaction
  is untested.** The fund prompt defaults to NEUTRAL because a directional
  call on a broad index is macro timing — the hardest call in markets. With
  funds holding 75% of the
  budget, a prompt that rarely takes a side may leave the account mostly in
  cash. Whether that is prudence or paralysis is a question for the journal,
  not for this page.
</Warning>

## Cost

`estimated_monthly_cost_usd()` prices **every** ticker through the full model,
which is what the list would cost with the [funnel](/orchestrator/llm) off:

| Watchlist size | Full model on every ticker |
|---|---|
| 35 | ~$242 |
| **80 (default)** | **~$553** |

That is the ceiling, not the bill. The funnel screens each ticker with a model
that costs about a tenth as much and stops there unless something is
happening, and the first live cycle measured **$0.25–0.40 for 35 tickers** —
so 80 comes to roughly **$0.60–0.90 a cycle, $13–19 a month**.

<Warning>
  Both figures rest on an assumed output length. The journal records measured
  `usage` on every call — use that instead, and see [Cost](/cost).
</Warning>

<Note>
  Time, not just money: a cycle costs roughly 24 seconds a ticker, so 80 names
  is a ~32-minute run. The heartbeat workflow's timeout was raised from 15
  minutes to 45 with this change — at 15 it would have been killed mid-run,
  after paying for every call it had already made.
</Note>

## Overriding it

```bash
WATCHLIST=GLD,RSP,MSFT python -m orchestrator.heartbeat --once
```

<Note>
  An override changes *what is evaluated*, never how it is sized. A ticker
  reached this way is still classified by `config/instruments.py`, so anything
  unrecognised gets the single-name cap and its own exposure group.
</Note>

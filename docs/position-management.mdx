title: "Position management"
description: "Sell a winner down in thirds, walk its stop up behind it, never loosen"
---

Until this existed, the engine opened positions and then never touched them
again. A trade was entered with a stop and left alone: the only way out was
that stop, so a loser left on schedule and a winner sat in the book
indefinitely. That is backwards, and once `MAX_OPEN_POSITIONS` fills, a book
full of old winners blocks every new signal.

Once per cycle, **before any new signal is judged**, every open position is
measured in **R** and walked up the ladder.

## R, and why the ladder is measured in it

R is the distance from the entry to the initial stop — what the trade risked.
A position at +1R has earned back exactly what it would have lost if stopped
out; at +3R, three times that.

Measuring in R rather than in percent is what lets **one rule fit every
instrument kind**: a 30%-volatility chip fund and a 0.5%-volatility bond fund
have very different stops, so "+1R" is a different number of dollars for each
— but the same statement about the trade.

## The ladder

`config/settings.py`:

```python
PROFIT_LADDER = (
    LadderRung(take_at_r=1.0, take_fraction=1 / 3, stop_to_r=0.0),
    LadderRung(take_at_r=3.0, take_fraction=1 / 3, stop_to_r=1.0),
)
```

| Rung | When | Sell | Stop moves to | The position afterwards |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | +1R | a third | the entry (breakeven) | can no longer lose |
| 2 | +3R | another third | +1R | is guaranteed a profit |
| — | — | the last third runs | stays at +1R | until the market takes it out |

Three properties the manager enforces around that table:

- **The stop only ever tightens.** A rung can raise it, the trail can raise
  it; nothing lowers it. A stop someone already moved higher keeps its height.
- **A runner always exists.** The fractions sum to less than one and are
  floored to whole shares, so arithmetic — not hope — guarantees the last
  share is never the ladder's to sell.
- **A gap is not a skipped rung.** A position that opens at +3.5R takes both
  rungs in the same cycle, in order.

A position too small to split (fewer than three shares) takes no tranche and
is managed by its stop alone; the ratchet still applies.

## The stop trails the price

Independently of the rungs, **every cycle the stop is trailed up** to
`price − ATR_STOP_MULTIPLIER × ATR` wherever that is tighter than where it
sits. That is the initial stop's own formula, re-anchored to today's price —
the same distance the entry was protected by, now measured from wherever the
trade has got to.

| Day | Price | Rung floor | Trail (price − 2×ATR) | Stop |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| entry | 100 | — | — | 96 |
| +0.5R | 102 | 96 | 98 | **98** |
| +1R | 104 | 100 (breakeven) | 100 | **100**, a third sold |
| +3R | 112 | 104 (+1R) | 108 | **108**, another third sold |
| +6R | 124 | 104 | 120 | **120** |

So the last third does not ride from +6R back to +1R waiting for a rung that
does not exist: the stop follows, and **the stop is the only exit**. The
rungs are floors the trail can never fall below; the trail is what keeps
pace between them.

Three things it will not do:

- **Loosen.** A widening ATR moves the candidate down; the ratchet ignores it.
- **Count as a rung.** A trailing raise carries no rung index, so the ladder's
  memory of which tranches are taken is untouched by it.
- **Fail the position.** An ATR so large the formula goes negative leaves the
  stop where it is and the position *held*, rather than erroring.

One check a day means the trail lags by up to a day; that is the cadence, not
a bug. A stop that ratchets daily on a two-ATR distance is a slow trail by
design — tight enough to keep most of a long run, loose enough not to be
shaken out by an ordinary pullback.

## What it cannot do, by construction

The manager reaches the broker through exactly four primitives:

| Primitive | Alpaca call | Can it open or enlarge a position? |
|---|---|---|
| read the live stop | `get_orders` | no |
| replace the stop | `replace_order_by_id` | no |
| close *part* of a position | `close_position(qty=N)` | **no — close-only by the broker's definition** |
| put a missing stop back | `submit_order(StopOrderRequest)` | **no — refused unless it can only reduce a position the book already holds** |

`DELETE /v2/positions/{symbol}?qty=N` reduces the named position and cannot
open, add to, or reverse one. That property is what lets a partial exit exist
at all without weakening the rule that every *entry* is a bracket with a
stop: a `MarketOrderRequest` without `stop_loss` is still never constructed
anywhere in the codebase, and CI checks both that and that these four calls
exist in `app/broker_client.py` and nowhere else.

A standalone stop is not close-only the way `close_position` is — a stop
*sell* for a symbol nobody holds would open a short. So the broker client
reads the book first and refuses the order unless the position exists, the
side is the one that closes it, and the quantity is no more than it holds.

<Warning>
  **A position with no live stop gets one back, and nothing else.** Until
  17 Sep 2026 it was reported as *unmanaged* and left exactly as found. That
  morning eleven of twelve open positions had no stop: the bracket's stop leg
  had been a *day* order, the broker cancelled it at the close, and "leave it
  alone" meant leaving it naked for the whole session. Now the manager places
  a stop at the level the audit log last recorded for the trade — the entry
  stop, or the last rung or trail that moved it — and, for a position with no
  record at all, at the entry formula's ATR distance from the last price,
  saying in the record that it was estimated. The ladder itself waits for the
  next pass. Every stop, the bracket's leg included, is now good-till-cancelled.
</Warning>

## Protect first, then sell

At every rung the stop is **replaced before** the tranche is sold, in one
atomic call that shrinks it to the shares it will still protect and moves it
up. Two reasons, one practical and one principled:

- A stop still sized for the old position reserves the shares the exit needs;
  the broker would refuse the sale.
- There must never be a moment in which the position is naked. A replace is
  atomic; a cancel-then-resubmit is not.

## Where the state lives

Nowhere new. The audit log — the system of record for what the engine did —
already carries the `ACCEPTED` record for each entry with its `entry_price`
and `stop_price`, so R is read from there. Every action the manager takes is
written to the same log as a `position_managed` record, and which rungs are
already taken is the count of those records since the entry.

That makes the ladder **idempotent under re-runs**: a second pass at the same
price sees the rung recorded and holds. A crash between "stop replaced" and
"tranche sold" heals on the next cycle — the rung is still due, the stop is
already where it should be, and the replace becomes a no-op.

If the entry record is missing (a position opened outside the engine, or a
lost log), R is estimated as `ATR_STOP_MULTIPLIER × today's ATR` — which is
exactly how the stop would have been set — and the record says
`r_estimated: true`.

## Slots: 40, and why that is not a diversity setting

A position that has taken both rungs holds a third of its size but a whole
slot. At the old limit of 20, a book of trimmed runners would have sat at
~30% of the account with the other 65% in cash and **no room to open
anything** — the count blocking exactly the redeployment the ladder exists
to free. `MAX_OPEN_POSITIONS` is 40, half the watchlist.

That does not put 40 positions on from day one: the gross ceiling still
fills at around 13–17 full-size entries. It lets the book *grow* as the
ladder frees cash.

<Note>
  Diversity was never the slot count's job. It comes from the limits that
  target concentration directly — 25% per exposure group across twenty
  groups, the sleeve budgets, the per-kind caps — and those hold at 20
  positions or 40. More slots can only make the book wider.
</Note>

## The conviction floor moved with it

`MIN_CONVICTION` went from 0.60 to **0.30** in the same change. Three live
cycles had produced some 120 signals and zero trades: the model's directional
calls landed between 0.30 and 0.58, and the floor sat above every one of them.

Lowering it makes **no single trade larger** — size comes from the cap and the
stop, never from conviction — and costs **no extra API spend**, because the
model is called either way. What it buys is the only thing a paper account is
for: trades, across a spread of convictions, so that
[scoring](/scoring) can say where the floor belongs instead of a constant
saying it. Until that data exists, the risk engine, this ladder and the stop
had never once run in live conditions.

## Running it by hand

```bash
python -m app.position_manager          # one pass, in this process
python -m app.position_manager --json
python -m app.position_manager --protect-only   # only put missing stops back
```

The protection pass is the one thing worth running after the close: a
good-till-cancelled stop placed overnight is live at the open. It skips the
market-hours gate, touches nothing that already has a stop, and exits
non-zero if any position is still without one when it is done. On GitHub,
run the **heartbeat** workflow by hand with `mode` set to `protect`; the
audit log it writes is committed like a cycle's.

Behind the webhook it is `POST /webhook/positions/manage`, same secret as the
signal endpoint, no body — there is nothing a caller could say that should
change what the ladder does.

The [daily report](/cycle-report) gains an **Open positions** section, read
from the same audit records, in plain words:

```
| Eli Lilly (LLY) · Company | Sold part. Sold 1 of 4 shares at +1.02R, 3 still held. Stop-loss raised 1079.13 → 1142.29. |
| Gold (GLD) · Commodity    | Holding. +0.31R, holding 12 shares. Stop-loss 310.50. |
| Exxon (XOM) · Company     | Stop placed. Had no stop-loss order, so a new one was placed at 96.00 (from the trade's own record). |
```

Sold and flagged rows come first; the merely-held ones after.

---
title: "What a Fund Reads Instead"
description: "An index has no P/E, no insiders and no analyst covering it — so it is asked six different questions instead of being handed a company's form with the answers blank"
---

Two thirds of the watchlist are funds. For a long time each one was described
to the model in the company's form: sector, market cap, profit margin,
revenue growth, next earnings date. An index has none of those, so the block
rendered as seven lines of `n/a`.

That was the better outcome. The worse one is that Yahoo sometimes fills a
blank in with nonsense — the first 80-ticker cycle showed the model a
**forward P/E of −4,036** for a Treasury bond fund, and the model duly scored
that fund's fundamentals −0.1.

<Note>
  A number the model cannot use is worse than a stated blank, because it looks
  usable. That sentence is the reason every section on this page exists.
</Note>

## The six sections

A fund's prompt omits `FUNDAMENTALS`, `ANALYST & INSTITUTIONAL VIEW` and
`INSIDER ACTIVITY` outright — not as "unavailable this cycle", which invites
the model to wonder what it missed, but as questions never asked. In their
place:

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="MACRO — orchestrator/macro.py" icon="cloud">
    The weather every fund trades in. The Treasury curve at three months,
    five, ten and thirty years; the slope of ten-year minus three-month; the
    dollar index; and the VIX.

    These are *prices*, so they say what traders think rather than what
    somebody summarised. The slope is there because a level cannot give it:
    an inverted curve is a regime, not a reading. Volatility is there because
    the same signal means different things at a VIX of 12 and at 34.

    The fund prompt tells the model to answer NEUTRAL unless something
    specific changed — "a policy or rate surprise, a data release well
    outside expectations" — and then used to hand it a day of headlines and
    ask it to judge whether a rate surprise had happened. It had no rates.

    With a FRED key configured the block carries two more lines: what the
    economy actually *did*, rather than what it is priced at. See
    [Official Data](/orchestrator/official-data).
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="FUND BASICS — orchestrator/funds.py" icon="layer-group">
    What the fund is, in terms that apply to a fund. Three shapes, because
    three kinds of fund answer three different questions:

    | shape | what it carries |
    | --- | --- |
    | `equity` | weighted P/E, P/B and P/S of the holdings, earnings growth, yield, expense ratio, net assets, asset mix, top names, sector mix |
    | `bond` | yield, credit-quality mix, share of government debt, three-year record, beta, cost and size |
    | `none` | nothing — the section is omitted |

    A 20-year Treasury fund and a high-yield credit fund are both "bonds" and
    behave nothing alike; the ratings mix is that difference stated plainly.
    A single commodity gets `none`: gold has no P/E, no yield and no balance
    sheet, and inventing a form for it would be the original mistake in a new
    costume.

    <Warning>
      **On trusting Yahoo.** The first live run returned a P/E of `0.06` for
      an energy fund — an earnings yield wearing a P/E's name, because Yahoo
      stores several holdings ratios inverted and lets its own formatter flip
      them back, while the `raw` field yfinance reads keeps them inverted.

      A suppression band written to catch *absurd* numbers does not catch
      this: `0.06` is not absurd, it is merely wrong, which is worse. So the
      parsing does not only reject — where the unit is knowable it converts,
      and where it is not it says nothing.
    </Warning>
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="ANALYST VIEW OF THE HOLDINGS — orchestrator/holdings.py" icon="users-viewfinder">
    Nobody publishes a price target on XLE. But XLE *is* a few dozen
    companies, every one of them covered — so the coverage is rolled up by
    the fund's own weights, built entirely from data already being fetched.

    The number that makes it honest is **coverage**: the share of the fund's
    weight that could actually be analysed. Five names at 62% of the fund is
    a statement about most of the fund; five names at 11% of a 500-stock
    index is not. The prompt says which it is, so the model can discount it.

    Top holdings only — fetching every constituent of a broad index would
    cost hundreds of calls per cycle for a progressively weaker signal. And
    the weights are the fund's while the ratings are the holdings': no
    attempt is made here to model the fund's own tracking, fees or structure,
    which is `funds.py`'s job.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="POSITIONING — orchestrator/positioning.py" icon="scale-unbalanced">
    A fund has no insiders. Nobody files a Form 4 on gold, and no director of
    the Treasury sells bonds and discloses it. But every large trader must
    report their futures position, and the CFTC publishes the aggregate every
    Friday — what the professional speculators hold long and short in gold,
    oil, wheat, Treasuries and the dollar.

    Three numbers, because the level alone is not a signal: **net as a share
    of open interest**, the **week-over-week change**, and its **percentile
    over the past year**. A crowded extreme is where positioning stops being
    confirmation and starts being a contrarian risk, and the model cannot
    know that from the level.

    The data is as of Tuesday and published Friday, so it is three to six
    days stale by the time a cycle reads it. That is the dataset, not a bug,
    and the prompt says so — along with the reminder that this is a crowding
    measure and not a forecast.

    <Warning>
      **Two ways a contract mapping goes wrong, and both were live.**

      *Dead.* A contract that stopped reporting is worse than one that never
      reported. Five mappings — every Treasury and the dollar index — were
      reading a series whose newest row was dated **February 2022**, printed
      as "positions as of" with no hint it was not this week's. A series
      whose newest row is older than a month is now treated as dead and the
      section omitted.

      *Mixed.* Worse, and invisible to that guard. The contract name used to
      be matched as a **substring**, so `GOLD` matched COMEX gold, micro gold
      and a Coinbase contract; the CFTC returned all three ordered by date;
      and the parser read consecutive rows as consecutive weeks. GLD printed
      "net long 0.0% of open interest" beside "change on the week −32.8%" —
      that change was the gap between two different contracts, and those
      12,246 contracts were Coinbase's open interest, not gold's. Nine of
      nineteen mappings were doing this.

      The name is matched **exactly** now. An exact name cannot interleave,
      and the next time the CFTC renames something the mapping goes silent
      and the staleness guard shows a gap — instead of quietly matching a
      neighbour and printing a confident number.
    </Warning>
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="COST OF HOLDING — orchestrator/carry.py" icon="arrow-trend-down">
    For a commodity fund this is arguably the most important fact about the
    instrument — more important than any forecast.

    A commodity fund does not hold the commodity. It holds futures, and every
    month it must sell the expiring contract and buy the next one. When the
    next month costs more, that roll loses money, every month, for ever. USO
    is the famous case: crude traded at the same price at both ends of a
    stretch in which USO lost most of its value.

    The measure is not the shape of the futures curve, which would have to be
    assembled from dated contract symbols that differ by commodity and roll
    under your feet. It is what the holder actually experiences:

    > the fund's return, minus the commodity's own return, over the same window

    Measured over three, six and twelve months. That gap *is* the cost of
    holding — roll losses and fees together, measured rather than modelled —
    and it works for a basket like DBC where no single pair of contracts
    would.

    Three limits, all stated in the prompt: it is **backward-looking** (it
    says what the structure has been costing, not what it will); it is **not
    a direction** (heavy carry is a reason to want a bigger move to justify a
    long, and a tailwind for a short); and a **physically backed fund has
    almost none of it** — GLD holds bullion, so its gap is roughly its fee,
    which is a real and useful difference from USO.

    <Note>
      **When the reference itself is the problem.** Yahoo's `CL=F` and friends
      are *continuous front-month* series: at each contract change the quoted
      price becomes the next contract's, and where the two differ the series
      steps without anyone having gained or lost.

      Gold suggests this is small — GLD against `GC=F` came back at −0.1% a
      year, about its fee, which could not happen if the steps were large.
      Crude did not: USO against `CL=F` measured **+55.9% a year**, at the
      very edge of what deep backwardation can produce and equally consistent
      with the reference stepping down at every roll.

      Faced with a number that could be either, the module does what the rest
      of the project does with a number it cannot corroborate: it does not
      show it. Past 25% a year the section is omitted and the prompt is
      shorter. A 15% drag on a grain fund is ordinary and survives; a claimed
      56% gain does not, because being wrong about that one would be worse
      than saying nothing.
    </Note>
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="FUND FLOWS — orchestrator/flows.py" icon="money-bill-transfer">
    Who is actually buying, measured in money rather than opinion.

    An ETF has no fixed share count: when demand exceeds supply an authorised
    participant creates new shares against a basket of the underlying, and
    when it falls away shares are redeemed and destroyed. **So the share
    count is the flow.** A fund whose shares outstanding grew 8% in a month
    had real money pushed into it by people who had to deliver the underlying
    to get in — and unlike a rating or a target, it has already happened.

    Three windows, because they answer different questions: a week is
    positioning, a month is a trend, a quarter is a regime. Quoted in dollars
    as well as percent, since 3% of a $70bn fund and 3% of a $400m fund are
    not the same event.

    <Note>
      **This one keeps its own history, and starts empty.** Nowhere free
      publishes a share-count *series* for an ETF — the live probe found
      Yahoo's fundamentals timeseries returning an empty series for every
      fund on this watchlist. What Yahoo does give is today's count.

      So one reading per fund per cycle is appended to `logs/fund_size.log`,
      which the heartbeat commits back to the repository alongside the
      journal. The series is the project's own memory. Below three readings
      the section is **absent** rather than present and saying nothing.
    </Note>
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## Absent is not failed

Every section above can be missing, and missing means two different things
that the prompt keeps apart:

- **Never on offer.** A bond fund has no crop condition, and an equity fund
  has no futures contract. The section is omitted outright and nothing is
  said about it.
- **Tried and failed.** A fetch that errored records a **named gap** in the
  prompt, so the model knows a thing it might have used is absent this cycle.

The difference is not cosmetic. It also decides what the trainer may learn
from: a score whose every source section was absent has nothing behind it,
whatever number the model wrote there, so the cycle records `null` rather
than the model's value. See [Scoring](/scoring).

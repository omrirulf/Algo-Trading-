Prices say what traders *think*. They do not say what happened. Four free
sources publish what happened, and each needs a key:

| section | source | key | who it is for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `MACRO`, last two lines | FRED (St. Louis Fed) | `FRED_API_KEY` | every fund |
| `ENERGY INVENTORIES` | EIA | `EIA_API_KEY` | oil and gas funds |
| `CROP CONDITION` | USDA NASS | `USDA_NASS_KEY` | grain funds |
| `EARNINGS RECORD` | Finnhub | `FINNHUB_API_KEY` | single names |

<Note>
  **A key is a way to see more, never a thing the system depends on.** With a
  key unset nothing is fetched and that section is simply not there — the same
  state as a ticker with no energy exposure. Running with none of the four is
  a supported configuration and is tested.
</Note>

## The sections

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="ENERGY INVENTORIES — orchestrator/energy.py" icon="oil-well">
    How much oil and gas is actually sitting in tanks. Every Wednesday the
    Energy Information Administration publishes what the United States is
    holding — and for an oil or gas fund this is not background, it is *the*
    scheduled event of the week, the one release that reliably moves the
    price. Until this existed the model was being asked to judge USO with a
    chart and a day of headlines.

    Four series:

    - **Crude** — commercial stocks, excluding the Strategic Petroleum
      Reserve. The headline number.
    - **Petrol** and **diesel** — what the refineries made of it. These move
      the refiners and the broad energy sector even when crude does not.
    - **Natural gas** — working gas in underground storage in the Lower 48.
      In summer the weekly figure is an injection and in winter a withdrawal,
      and the *sign flip* is the seasonal story.

    Three numbers each, for the same reason as the CFTC block: what is held,
    what changed on the week, and where that level sits against its own past
    year — because "424 million barrels" means nothing to anyone who does not
    already know the range.

    A build is more supply than demand and a draw is the reverse, so a build
    reads bearish and a draw bullish. The prompt says this is a rule of thumb
    rather than a law, and reminds the model that the market has already seen
    the number.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="CROP CONDITION — orchestrator/crops.py" icon="wheat-awn">
    A grain fund tracks one thing: how much of the crop there will be. The
    USDA publishes exactly that — weekly condition ratings through the
    growing season, county by county, aggregated nationally, free.

    The measure is the share of the crop rated **good or excellent**. It is
    the number the grain trade quotes, it is comparable week to week, and its
    *direction* is the signal: a crop deteriorating three weeks running is a
    supply story whatever the level.

    <Warning>
      **A seasonal source fails in a nastier way than a gap.** Condition is
      reported while the crop is in the ground and not at all outside that
      window — so out of season the section must be absent rather than
      showing a stale summer reading as though it were news.

      That much is straightforward. This is not: USDA labels winter wheat by
      its **harvest** year, so in September a request for 2026 returns
      `WEEK #47` — a week that has not happened yet in 2026, because the
      reading is from November of the year before. Printed as "week 47 of
      2026" it reads as the freshest number in the block while being ten
      months old.

      Every reading is therefore aged against the calendar, wrapping the
      year, and anything older than five weeks is dropped. That correctly
      leaves wheat with no section between seasons.
    </Warning>

    The prompt states the other limit plainly: these are published on a
    schedule everyone trades, so the edge, if any, is in the trend and the
    comparison to the same week last year — not in the number itself.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="MACRO, the FRED half — orchestrator/fred.py" icon="landmark">
    [`MACRO`](/orchestrator/fund-context) is four lines of prices without a
    key and six with one. The two extra lines are what the statistical
    agencies measured:

    | number | why it is here |
    | --- | --- |
    | **Inflation** | CPI year on year, requested as `pc1` so the percentage arrives already computed — deriving it here from index levels would make an off-by-one month invisible and wrong |
    | **Unemployment** | the rate; monthly, and the single most watched release there is |
    | **Jobless claims** | weekly first-time claims — the only one of these that is *fresh*; the others describe last month, this describes last week |
    | **Policy rate** | the upper limit of the Fed's target range: what the Fed has actually set, against which the three-month yield is the market's opinion |
    | **Breakeven** | what the market expects inflation to average over ten years, read off the gap between nominal and inflation-linked Treasuries — backward-looking CPI says what happened, this says what is priced |
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="EARNINGS RECORD — orchestrator/earnings.py" icon="receipt">
    Whether this company beats its own guidance, and by how much.

    The company block already carries what analysts *expect*. It said nothing
    about the one thing that predicts whether an expectation will be met: the
    company's own record of meeting them. A business that has beaten four
    quarters running and one that has missed four are priced by the same
    consensus and are not the same bet.

    Finnhub publishes the surprise history free — actual against estimate, by
    quarter — and covers the non-US lines on this watchlist, which is what
    decided it over the alternatives. ASML and TEVA both return full
    histories; that was checked against the live API rather than assumed.

    <Note>
      **It is a heading of its own, and that was the deliberate part.** This
      is the single addition to a single name's context since the replay and
      sanity baselines were set, so a company's prompt carries five headings
      without a Finnhub key and six with one.

      Hiding the extra lines inside `FUNDAMENTALS` would have kept the
      heading count unchanged, which was briefly the plan. It is the wrong
      trade: if the input is changing, the change should be visible in the
      shape of the question rather than buried in an existing answer. A test
      pins both lists.
    </Note>
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## One module reads the keys

Every fetch above lives in `orchestrator/sources.py`, and the parsing modules
stay what their siblings are — pure functions over payloads handed to them.

This is not tidiness. Two CI guardrails hold every module under
`orchestrator/` to reading no credentials, with a short allowlist for the ones
that must, because **these modules assemble the text sent to a language model,
and the signal journal they feed is committed to the repository every cycle.**
A key that reached a snapshot would reach a prompt, and then a public git
history.

Three rules, each with a test behind it:

- **A key goes into a request and nowhere else.** Never into a return value,
  never into a log line, never into an exception message that is logged. These
  APIs echo the query string back in their error bodies, so only exception
  *types* are ever recorded.
- **Nothing here formats or persists.** No rendering helpers, no file writes.
  A guardrail greps the file to keep it that way.
- **No key is a state, not a failure.** Every function returns empty and the
  caller omits the section.

## Checking them without guessing

None of this can be verified from a development container — the endpoints are
unreachable from it, and a mock proves only that the parser works on what the
test author imagined. The **data sources** workflow runs the real thing on a
GitHub runner:

```
Actions → data sources → Run workflow
```

It reports which spelling of each secret carried a key (never the key), then
renders each block exactly as the model would read it. A key that answers and
a section that renders correctly are two different things, and the fund block
taught us which one costs more to get wrong. See
[Continuous Integration](/continuous-integration).
